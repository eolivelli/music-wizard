# The sample corpus: real recordings, what is in them, and where they came from.
#
# Licensing is a gate, not a note. A file is committed only if it is our own or
# permissively licensed; anything else is gitignored and listed here with the
# command to fetch it, so a checkout without it is short of benchmarks rather
# than short of a licence. Attribution for the committed CC-BY files is in
# NOTICE as well as here.
#
# Chords are lead-sheet shorthand, spelled as the tool spells them: 7 dominant,
# m7 minor seventh, maj7 major seventh, m6 minor sixth, m7b5 half-diminished,
# dim diminished, a plain letter a major triad. One token is one bar, four bars
# to a line; X-Y is one bar holding both chords.
#
# Every grid below was confirmed against the recording by ear, and that is what
# lets it be ground truth: tools/score-samples.py scores it, and
# tools/baselines/score-samples.txt is the current reading. Where an entry is
# not scored it says why, and pop-c-g-am-f-120.mp3 says what a grid confirmed
# without ears is worth instead. Keys and tempos attributed to the uploader are
# claims, not measurements, and are marked as such.

eb7-vamp-130.mp3   (CC-BY, Guitar Improvisation, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ml4ICs8DKAE)
One-chord vamp, dominant quality, stated 130 bpm. The uploader's description
says two things the truth string cannot: the bass riff plays the major seventh
of the chord, and the comping riff is a tritone moving a half-step up, "D7 Eb7
or Ebm6 Eb7 depending on how you analyse it". So the minor third of the
written chord really is sounding, which is why this file is the one that
decides how a minor third is read (#272).

pop-c-g-am-f-120.mp3   (CC-BY, RainbowMonkey, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B13A7mPu_cs)
Pop backing track, stated C G Am F at 120 BPM in C major. THE pop progression,
and plain triads throughout, which is what makes it worth scoring: it is the
negative control for every quality the estimator can report (#273).

This is the one grid here that was confirmed without ears, and it is worth
knowing what that is worth. The stated 120 BPM in four is a two-second bar, the
recording is 114 seconds and the tracker finds 57 bars in it; the estimator
then names every bar's root correctly, which says only that it contradicts the
stated changes nowhere, and leaves open whether the recording holds anything
the four chords do not.

C G Am F

bossa-cm.mp3
Bossa nova in C minor, sixteen bars. The cycle measures 25.650 s, so a bar is
1.6031 s — fitted to the chroma over every cycle length and phase, and an
isolated winner among them (#302). The tracker's pulse is three eighths of that
bar, which is the comping pattern's 3+3+2 grouping rather than the quarter note,
so its bar spans about one and a half of the music's and there is no phase at
which its bars can be right (#231). Every column it scores is bounded by that
rather than by harmony.

Half its bars carry a chord that pitch class alone cannot tell from a chord on
another root: Fm6 and Dm7b5 are the same four notes, and so are Cm6 and Am7b5.
That is a ceiling on this recording rather than a defect, and it is #316.
  yt-dlp -x --audio-format mp3 -o samples/bossa-cm.mp3 \
    https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7H7Xg6U7P5g

Cm7    Cm7  Fm6     Fm6
Dm7b5  G7   Cm6     Cm6
Ebm7   Ab7  Dbmaj7  Dbmaj7
Dm7b5  G7   Cm6     Dm7b5-G7

The G blues that was the reference recording until #204: no licence and no
provenance were ever recorded for it, and the project's own tier-2 rule makes
saying so a gate, so it is no longer committed or scored. Figures citing it in
javadoc are measurements taken while it was, and stand as history rather than
as something a checkout can reproduce.
